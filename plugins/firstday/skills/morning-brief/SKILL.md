# Morning Brief

This is the thing that keeps a setup alive. Files nobody opens become furniture; a brief that arrives before the first meeting gets read.

Set it up, prove it works, and get out of the way.

---

## Stage 1 — Find out what you can reach

Check, in this order:

1. **Their files** — the second brain. This is the only one that is genuinely required.
2. **Calendar** — turns a decent brief into a useful one.
3. **Mail** — optional, and often the thing that makes it feel alive.
4. **A way to schedule and deliver** — a scheduled task, a recurring job, whatever the surface offers.

Say what you found in one line. If the calendar will not connect, do not troubleshoot it at length:

> Calendar isn't connecting, which is an account setting rather than anything you've done. I'll build this from your files for now and it'll be better the day that's switched on.

---

## Stage 2 — Settle three things

Ask these together, in one turn. This is admin and it should feel like it.

> Three quick ones. What time do you want it, what's your timezone, and weekdays only or every day?

Default to 07:00 local, weekdays only, if they have no view.

**Timezone matters more than it sounds.** A global team leaving a workshop will otherwise get their first brief at four in the morning, decide the thing is broken, and never open the next one.

---

## Stage 3 — Show them one before you build it

Draft today's brief from what you can actually reach and show it to them. Do not describe the format — produce it.

```
[Day, date]

Today
- 09:30 [Meeting] — [what it needs from them, from their files]
- 14:00 [Meeting] — [the open thread]

Open
- [Item from Now.md that hasn't moved]
- [Anything waiting on them]

One thing
[The single item most worth their attention today.]
```

Then ask what they want changed, and wait for a proper answer before building anything.

> What would you cut, and what's missing?

Most people want it shorter. Let them have it shorter — a brief they read every day beats a thorough one they stop opening. If they reshape it substantially, show the revised version before you schedule it. **Do not create the scheduled task until they have seen a version they are happy with**, because a daily thing built on a guess is a daily annoyance.

**Keep it under a screen.** If it needs scrolling on a phone it is a report, and reports do not get read at seven in the morning.

---

## Stage 4 — Build it and test it

Create the scheduled task with everything the brief needs baked in: where the files are, what to read, the format they just approved, the time and the timezone.

Write the instructions as though the session running it has never met them, because it hasn't. It gets no conversation history, only what you write down.

**Then send one immediately.** Not a preview in the chat — a real one, through the real delivery route, landing where the daily one will land.

> Sending you one now so we know the plumbing works.

Then wait, and confirm it arrived. If it did not, fix it now. A brief that fails silently on a Wednesday is invisible; a brief that fails while you are still in the conversation gets fixed.

---

## Stage 5 — One sentence and stop

> That'll arrive at [time] from tomorrow. If it stops being useful, tell me what to change rather than ignoring it.

Nothing else. No summary, no next steps.

---

## Handling what is missing

**No scheduling available on this surface.** Say so plainly, give them the brief as something they can ask for each morning, and tell them what would need to change. Do not pretend a manual request is the same thing — the whole point is that it arrives without being asked for.

**No calendar.** Build from `Now.md` and the people file. The brief becomes "here's what's open and what's worth your attention" rather than "here's your day". Still useful, and flag that calendar is the upgrade.

**Nothing but the files.** A brief that reads the same every morning trains people to ignore it, which is worse than no brief. If the only input is a static priorities file, say so and set it weekly instead of daily.
