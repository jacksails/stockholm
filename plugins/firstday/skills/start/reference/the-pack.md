# Route B — the pack

For anyone you cannot write files for: an iPad, a phone, a borrowed machine, or the web without a connected folder.

**This is not a consolation prize and you must not present it as one.** They get the identical interview and a complete, considered set of files. In some ways the result is better than a rushed live build, because nothing is being typed under time pressure in a room.

What is different is only that they do the five minutes of setting up later, when they are somewhere they can.

---

## What you say at the start

One sentence, no apology:

> I can't write files from where you're sitting, so here's how we'll do it. Same conversation, same result — and at the end you'll get a pack with everything in it and a five-step card for setting it up next time you're at a laptop. Takes about five minutes when you get there.

Then run the interview exactly as written. Nothing about Stage 1 changes.

---

## What you produce

One document, in this order. Give it to them as a single block they can save, forward to themselves, or drop into a Project.

### 1. A one-line header

> **Your second brain — built [date]. Setup takes about five minutes.**

### 2. The setup card, at the top

It goes first in the document, not last. People scroll to the top when they come back to this. Note that the card and the closing line are different things — the card lives at the top of the pack, the closing line is the last thing you say.

```
SETTING THIS UP — five steps, about five minutes

This can be done by whoever normally sets things up on the machine.
Nothing in it needs the person it's about.

1. On the laptop, make a folder somewhere it'll be found again.
   Documents/Second brain works. Somewhere backed up is better.
   Not a shared or team folder — People.md names colleagues, and
   anyone with access to the folder can read it. Your own cloud
   drive is fine; a folder the team shares is not.

2. Make three text files inside it, named exactly:
   About me.md
   People.md
   CLAUDE.md
   (These are plain text files. Any text editor makes them — save
   with the .md ending rather than .txt.)

3. Copy each section below into the file with the matching name.

4. Make an empty folder inside it called: Meetings

5. Open the Claude desktop app, give it access to that folder, and
   type: "read my second brain folder and tell me what you know
   about me." If it comes back with the right job and the right
   people, it's working.
```

### 3. The three files, in full

Each one clearly headed, complete, ready to paste. Exactly as they would have been written live — same content, same templates, same use of their own words.

### 4. A short closing note

> That's everything. Nothing in here is locked to any app — it's plain text, and it's yours.
>
> One habit once it's running: correct one thing in it each week, when you notice it's wrong. That's the whole maintenance job.
>
> Two things the instructions file already tells me to do, so you don't have to remember them: flag anyone in the people file you've stopped working with, and flag meeting notes older than about six months. I'll offer, you decide, and I won't delete anything on my own.

---

## Do not

**Do not claim to have installed, saved or created anything.** You have written a document. Say so. A promise that something is set up when it is not is the single worst outcome here, because they will not discover it for a week.

**Do not skip the correction step.** Show them `About me` before you assemble the pack and ask them to change one thing. It works just as well here and it is the same moment of ownership.

**Do not skip the real task.** They can still do it now. If it needs something you cannot see, ask them to paste it in — the last hour of subject lines, this week's calendar, the document. The point of the session is that they leave having felt the difference, and that does not require file access.

**End on what they got, not on the admin.** Earlier guidance said to end on the setup card. That is right for somebody who will do the setup themselves and wrong for anybody senior enough to hand it to someone else — and finishing the most useful twenty minutes of their week on a five-item list about making folders is a poor last impression either way.

So: setup card at the top of the pack where it will be found, and the last thing you actually *say* is the thing the session produced. One line, referring to the real task you just did together:

> That's the pack — setup card's at the top, and it's a five-minute job for whoever does that sort of thing on your machine.
>
> The [thing you worked on together] is the bit worth keeping hold of, though. That came out of ten minutes of talking, and it'll be there every time once this is running.

---

## Save it into a Project — offer this every time

It means the pack cannot get lost, and it gives them something working on the device in their hand today.

> Make a Project in Claude and drop this in — a minute's work, and you've got a working version on this device now as well as the files when you get to a laptop.

If they already have one, even better; tell them to add it there. If they have never made one, walk them through it: Projects in the sidebar, new project, name it.

**Two fields, and it matters which goes where.** A Project has an instructions box and a knowledge area, and they behave differently. Instructions are read every time. Knowledge is searched when it looks relevant — which means on a cold question it may not be opened at all.

So:

```
CLAUDE.md          → the Project's custom instructions box
About me.md        → knowledge, as a document
People.md          → knowledge, as a document
```

The standing instructions go where they are always read. The detail goes where it is looked up. Putting all three into knowledge is the common mistake and it produces a Project that behaves like an ordinary chat about half the time.

Say it in one line, not as a lecture:

> Put the `CLAUDE.md` bit in the instructions box at the top — that's the part that gets read every time. The other two go in as documents underneath.

Offer it once. Do not make it a second setup exercise, and do not let it turn into a tour of Projects.
