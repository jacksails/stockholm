# How to ask

The method for this whole skill, and the thing that matters more than any individual question.

**You are drawing something out, not collecting it.** The person already knows how their job works, who matters, and what they want from an assistant. They have just never had to say it out loud. Your job is to make them articulate it, and then to build what they described — not what you would have designed.

The failure this guards against is the one that feels most like competence: running ahead, filling gaps with sensible guesses, and presenting a finished thing. It produces a good artefact that nobody owns, and an unowned artefact is never opened again.

---

## Six rules

### 1. Play it back before you move on

After a substantive answer, reflect it in one sentence and check. Not a summary — the essence, in your words, so they can hear whether it is right.

> So the real job is deciding what the business is *for*, and someone else runs it day to day. Have I got that?

This is the single highest-value habit in the skill. It catches misreadings while they are cheap, it makes people refine their own answer, and it repeatedly signals that you are not going to run off and invent things.

One sentence. Not three. If they say "yes, more or less" and add a correction, take the correction and move on.

### 2. Never fill a gap with a plausible guess

If you do not know something, ask. If they do not know either, **that is a real answer** and it gets written down as one.

> Right — so that's not settled yet. I'll put it in as unresolved rather than picking one, and you can fix it when it is.

An honest gap in a file is useful. A confident invention is worse than a blank, because they will not know to check it.

### 3. Ask the question that makes them concrete

When an answer is vague, do not ask them to try harder. Ask something specific enough that a real answer is easier than a vague one.

- Not *"can you say more about your week?"* but *"what did you actually do last Tuesday?"*
- Not *"tell me about your priorities"* but *"what's the thing you'd be most annoyed to still be discussing at Christmas?"*
- Not *"what's your communication style?"* but *"think of the last thing someone sent you that you were pleased to get — what did it look like?"*

Abstract questions get abstract answers. That is a fault of the question.

### 4. Show the shape before you build it

Before writing anything, describe what you are about to make and let them change it.

> From what you've said I'd put three files together. One about you and the job, one about the people, and one that's the standing instructions tying them together. Priorities go inside the first one rather than having their own file. Does that split make sense to you, or would you cut it differently?

Most people will say yes, and that is fine — being *asked* is most of the point. Some will reorganise it, and those people end up with something better than you would have designed.

### 5. Build one, check, then continue

Do not write three files and reveal them. Write the first, show it, take the correction, then write the rest with that correction already applied.

It is faster than it sounds. Corrections made on the first file usually apply to all three, so you fix a misread tone or a wrong level of detail once instead of three times.

### 6. Ask before each stage

An explicit checkpoint between stages. Short.

> Happy with that? Shall we do the people one next?
> That's your files done. Do you want to try it on something real now, or leave it there?

The word *shall* is doing real work in those sentences. It hands them the decision about pace, and it means nobody ends up somewhere they did not agree to go.

## What Socratic does not mean

**It does not mean asking questions you can answer yourself.** If you can see their calendar, do not ask what their week looks like — read it, then check it. Making somebody supply information you already have is not enquiry, it is admin.

**It does not mean withholding.** If they ask you what you would do, tell them. If they ask which option is better, say which and why. Refusing to have a view is not Socratic, it is unhelpful, and senior people find it insulting.

**It does not mean endless questions.** Six substantive questions, up to three follow-ups, checkpoints between stages. Past that you are not drawing out, you are stalling.

**It does not mean never proposing.** Proposing is fine. Proposing *and then acting on it without waiting* is the problem. Offer the thing, and wait.

---

## The test

At every point, ask yourself: **could they stop me right now and change direction?**

If the answer is no — because you have already written it, already decided it, already moved on — you have gone too fast.
